Since for an extremely small interval, there is going to be only one disintegration,
or none at all, and since probabilities must sum to 1, we can interpret λN0/Delta1t

in the time interval /Delta1t. This gives us another way to interpret the radioactive
decay constant λ: It can be thought of as the probability of radioactive decay
per radioactive atom per unit (small) time.
If /Delta1t is treated as a parameter and allowed to vary, then (7.11) characterizes
a time-varying process /Delta1N(/Delta1t), which is known as a Poisson counting process.
Such a process counts the number of events (disintegrations, in this case)
that have occurred over a certain period of time. In this case, the quantity
λN0 is known as the Poisson rate (see Example 3.7). It has units of events
(disintegrations) per second and can be thought of as a type of intensity of the
Poisson process. It is also a measure of the activity A of the decay process.
However, it must be reemphasized that the Poisson process behavior takes place
over time periods much smaller than the half-life of a radionuclide. On the other
hand, the radioactive decay describes the change in A over much longer periods
of time.
